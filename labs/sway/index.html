How trunks and branches move

Every branch here is a bending beam joined to the one it grows from. Each branch rides on its parent and also pushes back on it, so the whole tree moves as one linked system. Nothing is a looping animation.

The model

  • Each branch bends in its first beam mode, with stiffness from its thickness and length (3EI/L³). This is the approach of Stam 1997 and Habel et al. 2009. A strongly bent branch swings round in an arc rather than stretching.
  • The equations are fully coupled: a swinging limb tugs on the trunk. A branching structure only links each branch with those below it, so this can be solved exactly every frame, in time proportional to the number of branches (Featherstone's factorisation for branched systems).
  • Wind pushes by drag on wood and leaves, using the wind relative to the moving branch. Leaves fold and streamline in strong wind. Inner foliage is sheltered by the outer crown.

What the research says, and what to try

  • Branches are dampers. Field measurements on a palm, an Italian cypress, hoop pine and eucalypts found that sway is not a simple back-and-forth, because branches swinging out of step with the trunk soak up energy (James, Haritos & Ades 2006). On a Douglas-fir, all the large branches had nearly the same frequency as the whole tree, which makes them work like tuned dampers (Spatz, Brüchert & Pfisterer 2007). Tap Frequencies to see the branches' own rates.
  • Pull test. Foresters measure sway by winching a tree over and letting go. Moore & Maguire found damping from 8% to almost critical with full crowns, and 1–8% once the branches were stripped. Removing crown raised the frequency, but only after at least 80% of the crown mass had gone (source). A silver maple in full leaf was hard to set swaying at all, and changed little until over 80% of its branches were removed (Arboriculture & Urban Forestry, 2014). Try Pull test, then use Prune and Leaves on and run it again.
  • Leaves lower a tree's sway frequency (Jackson et al. 2019, field data from many species).
  • Sway paths. Trees don't sway back and forth in a line. They loop in complex patterns. Scots pines at a forest edge swayed mostly across the wind rather than along it (Peltola et al. 1993), and moved less than 5 cm at mid-crown in 4–8 m/s winds. The path panel shows the trunk top from above. Here, in open ground, the along-wind motion is larger.
  • Higher modes live in branches. Analysis of real tree shapes shows many vibration modes bunched together, with the faster ones confined to particular branches (Rodriguez et al., walnut tree; scaling law).

Calibration

Natural frequencies come out at about 0.25–0.4 Hz with leaves, the range measured on real trees. Pull-test damping comes out at about 18–21% for broadleaves in leaf, 6% bare, and 5–9% for conifers. Big oaks have thick trunks and bend little even in a gale; their crowns do the moving. Slender trees like birch and poplar bend visibly. Turn on Rest shape to see how far.
