How the water is simulated

The ripples

The pond's surface is a 256 × 256 grid of 2.5 cm cells. Waves on it follow the real dispersion of water waves: long ones travel faster than short ones, and very short ones are pulled along by surface tension. That is why a stone's ring spreads into a widening train of ripples.
  • How it's computed. Each 1/60 s step is solved exactly in Fourier space, as in Tessendorf's eWave (2014), extended with surface tension and the pond's depth.
  • Obstacles. Reed stems, jetty posts and the sleeper wall hold the water still where they stand, as in iWave (Tessendorf 2004). Waves reflect off them and bend round them.
  • Where waves lose energy. The reed bed drains energy as water drags past the stems, the shallow beach on the west soaks waves up, and lily pads calm the water under them. Pond water usually carries a thin surface film, which damps short ripples far faster than clean water does. You can set this with the Surface slider.
  • Checks against theory. A wave packet travels at its theoretical group speed and comes back off the wall with about 89% of its energy.

The golf ball

  • Skip or plunge. As the ball crosses the surface, each bit of its skin that pushes into the water feels the water's impact pressure. This is the classical ricochet model (Johnson & Reid 1975; Hutchings 1976). Tuned to one constant, it gives the measured critical angle: a sphere won't skip above 18°/√SG. A golf ball's SG is about 1.13, so that's about 17°. Below that, a fast ball skips, the Masters' 16th-hole party trick. Above it, the ball goes in.
  • The cavity. A plunging ball drags an air cavity down behind it. Each slice of the cavity, opened as the ball passed, swells and then collapses under the water's weight. The cavity pinches off about halfway down, after a time ≈ 2^(5/4)·√(R/g), about 0.11 s for a golf ball (Duclaux et al. 2007; Aristoff et al. 2010).
  • Jet and bubbles. The collapse throws up a Worthington jet (Gekle & Gordillo 2010), and the lower cavity breaks into bubbles.
  • Sinking. The ball, 13% denser than water, sinks at about 0.4 m/s.
  • Splash curtain. The splash rises round the hole. On fast entries it is pulled in and can seal over the cavity.

Rain

Raindrops arrive at the rate and sizes given by the Marshall–Palmer drop-size distribution, falling at their terminal speeds. Each drop makes a small crater that spawns ring waves holding about 1% of its energy; ring energy grows with drop energy (Bliven et al.). The biggest drops go into the simulation, and the many small ones are drawn as rings.

Wind

Clean water stays glassy until the wind reaches about 1.3 m/s (Kahma & Donelan 1988). Above that, ripples grow with the wind and arrive in gusty patches, the cat's paws. The water stays calm in the lee of the reeds, the jetty and the banks, for several times their height. Floating leaves drift at about 3% of the wind speed.

What's simplified

  • Grid size. The grid can't hold ripples shorter than about 5 cm, so the smallest rain rings are drawn rather than simulated.
  • Splash and jet. The splash curtain's shape and the jet's height are scaled from the literature rather than simulated as free-surface flow.
  • Backspin. It barely changes the skip angle here, though experiments find it helps a ball skip.
