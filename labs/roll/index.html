How a golf ball lands and rolls

The flight is the game's own model, calibrated to TrackMan PGA Tour averages. The interesting part is what happens when the ball meets the turf. It's simulated through the impact itself, in steps of a few microseconds, rather than with a rule for the rebound.

The turf

  • Elastic, then it gives. The ball is far stiffer than turf, so the turf does the deforming (Haake modelled it as visco-elastic layers). Here the turf pushes back like a spring until the pressure under the ball passes the soil's bearing strength. Then it gives way for good. That permanent dent is the pitch mark. Soft, wet ground gives way sooner, so the ball sinks deeper, bounces less and can plug.
  • The crater pushes back. As the ball sinks while moving forward, it runs into the front of its own pitch mark, so the turf effectively pushes back against the motion. Penner (Can. J. Phys. 80, 2002) modelled this as a tilted surface. Here the tilt grows as the ball sinks.
  • Grip and spin. The contact has some give sideways as well as friction. Analysis of a hard spinning ball on a compliant surface shows that this give is what lets a ball check or spin back (point-contact models of the bounce; Cross, Bounce of a golf ball on the green).
  • Firm vs soft greens. Haake fired spinning balls onto 18 UK courses' greens (721 impacts). On some greens balls came off with topspin; on others they kept their backspin, if they landed with more than about 350 rad/s (about 3,300 rpm). Backspin survived on freely drained greens with less than 60% Poa annua (Haake 1989). Harder turf lets the ball slip right through the impact (Haake 1998). Here, spin is rubbed off in proportion to how deep the ball sinks, so soft or wet greens kill spin and firm ones keep it.
  • Grass. Blades hold the ball up; it sits down further in longer, sparser grass. Grass also drags on the ball in proportion to its speed and how deep it is in the grass, which is why rough stops a ball.

Rolling

  • Once the bouncing stops, the ball skids until its spin matches its speed, then rolls. Rolling deceleration on a green isn't constant: the retarding force grows as the ball slows (rolling friction studies). Here it rises at low speed.
  • Green speed is set by the Stimpmeter: the ball leaves the bar at about 1.83 m/s, and the distance it rolls, in feet, is the green speed. Try Stimpmeter test.

Calibration

The few turf constants were fitted to tour roll-out on a normal fairway: driver about 20 yd, 7-iron about 5, pitching wedge about 2, with irons holding a normal green. The results:
  • A drive rolls about 21 yd on fairway, 15 in the 1st cut, 7 in the 2nd, 2 in the 3rd and under 1 in long rough.
  • On links-firm ground the drive runs about 29 yd.
  • On a normal green a PW stops dead.
  • Wet ground takes the spin off.
  • On sodden fairway, irons plug.
