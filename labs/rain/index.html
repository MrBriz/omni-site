How rain turns into wet ground

  • Leaves and grass catch it first. A tree in leaf holds about 1.2 mm and a fairway sward about 0.3 mm. The rest drips through (Rutter et al. 1971; Gash 1979). A short shower can soak the leaves and grass without reaching the soil.
  • Soaking in. Water soaks in at the Green–Ampt rate, fast at first and slowing as the wetting front goes deeper (Green & Ampt 1911; Mein & Larson 1973; soil values from Rawls et al. 1983). Rain faster than that fills the hollows, and the overflow runs off.
  • Draining. The top 150 mm drains into the subsoil at its unsaturated conductivity (van Genuchten 1980; Mualem 1976). Sand is back to normal within hours. Clay holds on for days, and once its subsoil is full the top stays waterlogged with puddles. That's why a week of rain is so different from a shower.
  • Drying. Everything dries by evaporation at the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith rate (Allen et al. 1998), worked out from temperature, humidity, wind and sunshine at Milton Keynes' latitude. That's ~4 mm a day in a sunny week and ~0.2 mm in winter. Clear, still nights leave dew.
  • Greens and bunkers are built over gravel and drain freely. Greens are watered at night when they dry out.
